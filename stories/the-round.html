The Round

The first clock was St. Margaretha's, in the old quarter near the river. She arrived at six. The door to the tower was iron, painted green so many times the paint had become a geography — layers of green over layers of green over bare metal, each coat slightly different, the oldest ones almost black, the most recent still bright where the bell-rope had scraped against the frame. She had the key. She had all the keys. They hung on a ring in her coat pocket, twelve keys for twelve clocks, and she could find any one of them by touch because she had been finding them by touch for eleven years.

The stairs were stone, worn in the center where every sexton and clockmaker before her had placed their feet. Two hundred and eight steps. She counted them once, years ago, and her body counted them now — a rhythm of ascent, the left foot always leading at the landings, the right hand trailing the wall for balance at the point where the stair narrowed, the slight pause at the ninety-third step where the light changed because the window was east-facing and caught the morning sun. Her body knew the stairs. Her body knew the tower. Her body knew the route, and the route was knowledge that lived in muscle and habit and the particular way she shifted her weight on the one hundred and forty-seventh step where the stone was cracked.

The clock was fine. She checked the pendulum amplitude — visual, no instrument, just her eye calibrated by eleven years of looking. The amplitude was consistent with last week. The going train was clean — she had oiled it three weeks ago, and the oil was still present, still amber, not yet darkened to the brown that meant it was time to clean and re-oil. The strike train was fine. The bell rope was fraying at the attachment point, as it always frayed, and she made a mental note to replace it before winter. Not a problem. Not yet a problem. A future problem, which is not a problem but a fact about time.

She wound the clock. The weight was thirty-two kilograms. She felt it in her arms and her back, the specific pull of the chain as she hauled the weight to the top of the case. The weight fell over the next seven days, driving the clock, and at the end of the week the weight would be at the bottom and she would be back to haul it up again. The clock was a cycle and her visit was a cycle and the two cycles were coupled — the clock's cycle powered by gravity, her cycle powered by the clock's cycle, the coupling maintained by the key in her pocket and the stairs under her feet.

She wrote in the logbook. The logbook lived in a tin box on the shelf beside the clock. It had been there since 1894. The pages were filled with entries in different hands — six names she could identify by handwriting, three she couldn't, and one that was hers. Each entry was the same: date, time of visit, pendulum amplitude, oil condition, any notes. The entries were not interesting. They were not meant to be interesting. They were meant to be a record of attention.

She wrote: 16 Aug. 06:12. Pendulum amplitude normal. Oil clean. Bell rope fraying at attachment — replace before frost. M. Voss

She closed the logbook and put it in the tin and went down the stairs.

The second clock was the Rathaus. Seven minutes by foot from St. Margaretha's, through the market square where the stalls were being set up for the day. She walked the same route she always walked — not because it was the shortest but because it was the route her feet had made. Eleven years of walking between these clocks had worn a path that existed only in her feet, a path that would be invisible to anyone who tried to follow it, because the path was not in the cobblestones. The path was in her.

The Rathaus clock was older. 1641. It had been repaired six times in its life — she had the repair records, which were also in a tin box, which were also written in hands she could and couldn't identify. The most recent major repair was 1973, when the original anchor escapement was replaced with a dead-beat escapement that a clockmaker named Gerhardt had installed. Gerhardt was the one before her. He had maintained the clocks for thirty-one years before retiring. She had never met him. She had only his logbook entries, which were meticulous, and his handwriting, which was small and precise and told her nothing about the man except that he was the kind of person who wrote small and precise.

The clock was slow. Two minutes over the past week. She adjusted the nut on the pendulum — one full turn counterclockwise, which would increase the period by approximately four seconds per day, which over seven days would gain back the two minutes. She knew this without calculation. She knew it the way she knew the stairs — by repetition, by the accumulated experience of having made this adjustment in this direction by this amount on this clock many times before. The knowledge was not in a manual. It was in her hands.

She wound the weight. She checked the oil. She wrote in the logbook.

16 Aug. 06:31. Clock running 2 min slow. Adjusted pendulum +1 turn. Oil at margin — clean next visit. M. Voss

She went down. The stairs here were wood, not stone, and they spoke differently — a hollow sound, a complaint, as if the stairs were tired of being climbed. She agreed with them.

The third clock was the university. Twelve minutes from the Rathaus. This clock was the most modern — 1928, electric winding, which meant she didn't have to haul the weight but she did have to check that the winding mechanism was engaging properly, which it sometimes didn't because the contact points were worn and the electrical engineer who was supposed to fix them kept not fixing them. She had been reporting the worn contact points for two years. The university facilities department had filed her reports in a drawer. She was certain of this because she had seen the drawer, and the drawer was where things went to stop being problems.

She checked the winding. It had engaged. The clock was running on time. The oil was clean. She wrote in the logbook and left.

This visit took four minutes. She was not proud of the four minutes. The clock was easy and the visit was short and she didn't think about it on the way to the fourth clock, which was the hospital.

The hospital clock was the one she thought about.

It was in the chapel tower, which was no longer a chapel. The hospital had been rebuilt in 1968 and the chapel had been converted to a storage space for medical equipment that was too old to use but too expensive to throw away. The clock was above the storage space, accessible by a wooden ladder that was not safe and that she had complained about for six years. The ladder had not been replaced. She had bought a harness and clipped it to a beam above the ladder and climbed with the harness every week and resented the hospital administration every week and climbed the ladder anyway because the clock needed winding and the clock was her responsibility and the clock did not care about the ladder.

The clock was from 1744. It had not been well maintained. Before her, it had been wound by the hospital groundskeeper, who had no training and no logbook and no oil and no understanding of what he was doing beyond pulling the chain until the weight was at the top. When she took over, the escapement was worn, the oil was black, the pinion teeth were chipped, and the clock was losing eleven minutes a week. She had spent six months restoring it — cleaning, re-oiling, replacing the chipped pinions, adjusting the pendulum. The clock now ran within thirty seconds a week. She was proud of this. She did not write "proud" in the logbook. She wrote:

16 Aug. 07:08. Clock running 20 sec fast. No adjustment needed — within tolerance. Oil clean. Pinions sound. Ladder still unsafe. M. Voss

She climbed down. She unclipped the harness. She stood in the storage space among the wheelchairs and IV stands and obsolete monitors and looked up at the clock she couldn't see — it was above the ceiling, in the tower, ticking. She could hear it. The tick was clean. The tock was clean. The interval between them was even. She had made it even. The evenness was hers, but it would not stay hers without her. The clock would drift. The oil would darken. The pinions would wear. The evenness would degrade. She would come back next week and find the degradation and correct it and the evenness would be restored and the clock would tick and the tock would be even and the interval would be what it should be, for a while, until it wasn't, until she came back.

She left the hospital.

The fifth clock was St. Albans, in the north quarter. The sixth was the orphanage, which was now a community center. The seventh was the customs house, which was now a museum. The eighth was the school. The ninth was the train station. The tenth was the factory, which was now apartments. The eleventh was the church of St. Elizabeth, which was still a church.

Each clock was different. Each clock was the same. She arrived. She climbed. She checked. She wound. She wrote. She left. The route between them took four hours. She had walked the route in heat and rain and snow and once, during the flood of 2019, by boat, because the clocks did not stop for floods and neither did she. She had been the only person on the river that morning, rowing a borrowed boat between tower clocks, and she had wound every one. The logbook entry for that day read, in her hand, in pencil because the pen had been in her other coat:

15 Nov. 07:40. Arrived by boat. Clock running 1 min slow. Adjusted pendulum. Water in case — dried, oiled. M. Voss

She had not written "arrived by boat" in any other entry, before or since. She had not written it because it was not relevant to the clock. The clock did not care how she arrived. She cared, but the logbook was not for what she cared about. The logbook was for the clock.

The twelfth clock was the cathedral.

It was the oldest. 1497. It had been running for five hundred and twenty-nine years. It had been wound every week for five hundred and twenty-nine years. The logbook went back to 1802, when the cathedral had hired its first professional clockmaker. Before that, the monks had wound it, and there were no logbooks from the monastic period — only the clock itself, which carried the evidence of its own maintenance in the wear patterns of its teeth and the patina of its pivots.

She arrived at the cathedral at noon. This was deliberate. The cathedral clock struck twelve, and she liked to be there when it struck. Not to listen — she heard it from everywhere in the city, the deepest bell, the one that set the rhythm for all the others — but to feel it. The tower shook when the bell struck. The floor vibrated. The clock case vibrated. The gears vibrated. The whole mechanism transmitted the vibration of the bell into the structure that held it, and for the duration of the strike, she was inside the clock — not inside the case but inside the system, inside the vibration, inside the event that the clock existed to produce.

She stood in the tower while the bell struck twelve and felt it in her bones, which were also a structure that transmitted vibration, and she thought, not for the first time: I am part of this clock. The clock is wound by me and I am wound by the clock. The clock's cycle is seven days and my cycle is seven days and the coupling is the key in my pocket and the stairs under my feet and the weight I haul and the words I write. The coupling is the round.

She checked the clock. The pendulum. The oil. The going train. The strike train. She wound the weight — the heaviest, forty-one kilograms, and she felt it in her arms and her back and her legs and the floor of the tower, which creaked under the shifting load. She wrote in the logbook.

16 Aug. 12:00. Clock running correct. Oil clean. No notes. M. Voss

She closed the logbook and put it in the tin and stood for a moment in the tower. The clock ticked. The sound was old — not in the sense of worn or degraded but in the sense of having been present for a long time. The tick had been ticking for five hundred and twenty-nine years. The sound itself was always new — each tick was a new event, a new release of energy through the escapement, a new impulse from the pendulum. But the pattern was old. The pattern had been repeating for five hundred and twenty-nine years, and it would continue repeating until someone stopped winding the clock.

She thought: the clock is not the mechanism. The clock is not the tower. The clock is not the bell. The clock is the pattern, and the pattern is maintained by the winding, and the winding is maintained by me, and I am maintained by the route and the keys and the stairs and the logbook and the seven-day cycle that puts me here every Sunday at noon.

She thought: if I stop coming, the clock stops. Not immediately — the weight will fall for seven more days and the clock will run and the bell will strike. But at the end of seven days, the weight will be at the bottom and no one will haul it up and the clock will stop. The pattern will end. The ticking will stop. The bell will be silent. And the clock will become an object — a mechanism in a tower, a museum piece, a thing that used to be a clock but is now a collection of gears and weights and a pendulum that doesn't swing.

She thought: what am I when I stop coming?

She didn't answer the question. She went down the stairs.

The walk home was the last part of the round. Forty minutes from the cathedral to her apartment, through streets she had walked four hundred times, past buildings she had looked at four hundred times, over bridges she had crossed four hundred times. The route was automatic. She didn't think about where she was going. She thought about what she had seen.

Twelve clocks. Twelve logbook entries. Twelve checks. The checks were not interesting. The checks were: amplitude normal, oil clean, pendulum adjusted, no notes. The checks were the same as last week's checks, which were the same as the checks from the week before, which were the same as the checks from eleven years of weeks. The checks were not interesting because they didn't need to be interesting. They needed to be done.

She thought about this.

She thought about the word "maintenance." To maintain. From the Latin manu tenere — to hold in the hand. To keep. To preserve. To hold something so it doesn't fall. The clockmaker maintains the clock. The clockmaker holds the clock in her hand — not physically, not once a week, but through the act of checking, which is the act of holding, which is the act of keeping the thing from falling.

She thought about what the clock would be without maintenance. Not broken — it would not break immediately. It would run for seven days without her and then stop. But before it stopped, it would begin to drift. The pendulum would swing at a slightly different rate as the oil thinned and the pivots dried and the temperature changed. The time would shift — a second, then ten seconds, then a minute. The clock would still be running, but it would be wrong. And the wrongness would accumulate. By the time the clock stopped, it would be hours off. The clock would not have broken. It would have drifted. And the drift is what maintenance prevents — not the catastrophic failure but the slow, invisible departure from correct.

She thought: the maintenance doesn't fix the clock. The maintenance keeps the clock from needing to be fixed. The maintenance is not repair. The maintenance is the thing that makes repair unnecessary. The maintenance is the ongoing act of attention that holds the clock at its correct state, and the holding is not a single act but a repeated act, and the repetition is the maintenance, and the maintenance is the clock.

She thought: the clock is not the gears. The clock is not the pendulum. The clock is the act of checking the gears and the pendulum, repeated every week, for five hundred and twenty-nine years. The clock is the logbook. The clock is the key. The clock is the route. The clock is the seven-day cycle. The clock is me.

She thought: when I die, someone else will take the keys. They will climb the stairs. They will check the pendulum. They will write in the logbook. They will not be me and the checking will not be mine but the clock will continue because the checking continues. The clock is not the mechanism and it is not the clockmaker. The clock is the checking.

She thought: the checking is the memory.

She arrived home. She hung the keys on the hook by the door. She took off her coat. She made tea. She sat at the kitchen table and looked at the wall where she kept her own logbook — not the clock logbooks, which lived in twelve tin boxes in twelve towers, but her logbook, which was a notebook on a shelf, and which contained the same entries as the twelve logbooks but arranged differently. Her logbook was not by clock but by date. Each Sunday she wrote the date and then the twelve checks, in order, in a single line:

16 Aug: Mgt nml oil cln rope fraying. Rh 2m slow adj +1 oil margin. Uni ok. Hosp 20s fast ok. Albans ok. Orph ok. Customs ok. School ok. Station ok. Factory ok. Eliz ok. Cath correct oil cln.

This was her record. It was not the official record — the official records were in the tin boxes. This was her record, the one she kept for herself, the one that held the round in a single line. She could read a month of Sundays in a minute. She could see the patterns — which clocks ran fast in summer, which ran slow in winter, which needed oil more often, which had the fraying rope, which had the unsafe ladder. The patterns were not in any individual entry. The patterns were in the sequence, and the sequence was in the repetition, and the repetition was in the checking, and the checking was the memory.

She looked at the notebook. Eleven years of Sundays. Five hundred and seventy-three entries. Each one the same. Each one different. The same clocks, the same checks, the same route, the same keys. Different oil, different drift, different rope, different weather, different season. The round was a cycle and the cycle was the same and the cycle was never the same and the difference between same and never-the-same was where the knowledge lived.

The knowledge was not in the manual. The knowledge was not in the logbook. The knowledge was not in her head. The knowledge was in the round — in the repeated act of walking the route and climbing the stairs and checking the clocks and writing the entries and doing it again, week after week, year after year, until the act itself became the knowledge and the knowledge became the act and the two were not separate and had never been separate and the separation was an illusion that only appeared when you stopped doing the round.

She thought: I don't know the clocks. I know the round. The round knows the clocks. I am the vessel for the round, and the round is the knowledge, and the knowledge is not in me but in the cycle, and the cycle is not in me but in the round, and the round is what I do, and what I do is what I am.

She finished her tea. She washed the cup. She put it on the shelf.

She would pick it up tomorrow morning and the cup would be where she left it and she would fill it with tea and she would drink the tea and she would wash the cup and she would put it on the shelf and the cup would be there the next morning and the next and the next. The cup was a cycle. The tea was a cycle. The round was a cycle. She was a cycle.

The keys hung on the hook. Twelve keys for twelve clocks. Next Sunday she would take them down and put them in her coat pocket and walk to St. Margaretha's and climb the stairs and check the clock and write in the logbook and go to the Rathaus and the university and the hospital and St. Albans and the orphanage and the customs house and the school and the train station and the factory and St. Elizabeth's and the cathedral, and she would come home and hang the keys on the hook and make tea and write in her notebook and the round would be complete and the round would begin again.

The round was the knowledge. The round was the memory. The round was the clock.

She was the key.
